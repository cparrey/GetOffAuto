Get Off Auto

    Every photo is just light, measured.

    Your camera only does three things to light: it lets in a certain amount, for a certain time, and records it with a certain sensitivity. Learn those three and every button starts to make sense.

    The exposure triangle

    Exposure means how bright or dark your photo comes out. Three settings control it, and each one also changes how the photo looks. That second part is the real reason to learn them.

    Aperture: how wide the hole is

    Inside the lens is an opening that can get bigger or smaller, like the pupil of your eye. It's written as an f-number: f/1.8, f/4, f/8, f/16.

    The numbers work backwards. Small number = big opening = more light. Big number = tiny opening = less light.

    What it changes in the picture: how much is in focus. A wide aperture (f/1.8–f/2.8) blurs the background behind a person. A narrow one (f/8–f/16) keeps everything sharp, from the flowers at your feet to the mountains.

    f/2.8

    Shutter speed: how long the light gets in

    The shutter opens for a moment and then closes. That moment is written in fractions of a second: 1/1000 is very quick, 1/60 is fairly slow, 2" means two whole seconds.

    What it changes in the picture: movement. A fast shutter freezes a kid jumping or a bird flying. A slow shutter blurs movement, which can look broken (shaky hands) or beautiful (silky waterfalls, light trails from cars).

    Rule of thumb: if you're holding the camera by hand, keep the shutter at 1/60 or faster, and faster still with long zoom lenses.

    ISO: how sensitive the camera is

    ISO is how strongly the camera amplifies the light it receives. ISO 100 is low and clean. ISO 3200 is high: brighter, but grainier.

    What it changes in the picture: noise, or grain. Use low ISO when there's plenty of light, and raise it when it's dark and you can't open the aperture any wider or slow the shutter any further.

    On film cameras, ISO is decided by the film you load. A roll of ISO 400 film is ISO 400 for all 24 or 36 shots.

    Stops: the one idea that ties it all together

    A stop means doubling or halving the light. Going from 1/250 to 1/125 is one stop more light. So is f/5.6 to f/4, or ISO 200 to ISO 400. Because they all use the same unit, you can trade between them: make the shutter one stop faster to freeze action, open the aperture one stop to make up for it, and the brightness stays the same.

    Full stops worth memorising: f/1.4 · 2 · 2.8 · 4 · 5.6 · 8 · 11 · 16 · 22. Each one lets in half the light of the one before. Your camera also shows the in-between thirds, like f/3.2 and f/3.5.

      The mode dial

      The mode dial decides who makes which decision. Each step from Auto to M hands one more choice from the camera to you. Tap a mode to turn the dial.

      Which mode should a beginner use?

      Start in A (Av on Canon). It's the mode most working photographers use day to day. You pick the aperture, which decides the look of the photo, and the camera handles the shutter speed. Glance at the shutter number now and then: if it drops below 1/60, raise the ISO.

      Once that feels natural, try S for anything that moves, and M when the light isn't changing, like a sunset or a night scene on a tripod.

      Focal length: how zoomed in you are

      Focal length is the number in millimetres on your lens, like 18–55mm or 50mm. Smaller number = wider view. Bigger number = closer, narrower view. Stand still and drag the slider.

      50mm

      Crop sensors, in one paragraph

      Most beginner cameras have a sensor smaller than a frame of 35mm film. It sees only the middle of what the lens projects, so every lens looks more zoomed in: about 1.5× on Nikon, Sony and Fujifilm, 1.6× on Canon. A 50mm lens on these cameras frames like a 75–80mm. Film cameras and "full-frame" digital cameras have no crop.

      Zoom lenses and prime lenses

      Zoom (e.g. 18–55mm, 70–300mm)

      One lens covers many focal lengths. Flexible and convenient. Usually doesn't open as wide, so it needs more light.

      Prime (e.g. 35mm, 50mm f/1.8)

      One fixed focal length; you zoom with your feet. Usually sharper and opens much wider. A 50mm f/1.8 is the best-value upgrade for most beginners.

      Reading what's written on a lens

      18–55mm 1:3.5–5.6 means it zooms from 18 to 55mm, and its widest aperture is f/3.5 at 18mm, shrinking to f/5.6 when zoomed in. 50mm 1:1.8 is a prime that opens to f/1.8. Letters like IS, VR, OSS or OIS mean built-in image stabilisation, which helps you hold slower shutter speeds by hand.

      Focal length and faces

      Wide lenses up close stretch features: noses look bigger, foreheads bulge. For flattering portraits, step back and use 50–135mm. It isn't the lens itself causing distortion; it's how close you are standing.

      Other settings, in plain English

      Menus are full of abbreviations. These are the ones that actually matter. Tap any one to open it.

      Exposure compensation (+/− button)

      Tells the camera "brighter than you think" or "darker than you think" in P, A, S and Auto ISO. It's measured in stops: +1 is twice as bright.

      Cameras try to make everything medium grey, so snow and white beaches come out dull (use +1) and dark scenes or sunsets come out washed out (use −0.7 to −1). Remember to set it back to 0 afterwards.

      Focus modes: AF-S, AF-C, MF
      AF-S / One Shot
      Focuses once when you half-press the shutter, then locks. For things that stay still: people posing, landscapes, food.
      AF-C / AI Servo
      Keeps refocusing while you hold the half-press. For anything moving: kids, pets, sport, birds.
      AF-A / AI Focus
      The camera guesses which of the two you need. Fine to start with, but less predictable.
      MF
      Manual focus: you turn the focus ring yourself. Useful in the dark, through glass, or for close-ups. Turn on "focus peaking" if your camera has it.

      Half-press: press the shutter button halfway to focus and measure light, then press fully to take the photo. This one habit fixes most blurry pictures.

      Focus points and areas

      Your camera can focus on many spots in the frame. If you leave it on automatic area, it usually picks whatever is closest, which isn't always what you wanted. Switch to a single point and move it onto your subject, or turn on eye detection for people and animals if your camera has it.

      Metering: matrix, centre-weighted, spot
      Matrix / Evaluative / Multi
      Measures the whole frame and makes a smart guess. Leave it here most of the time.
      Centre-weighted
      Pays most attention to the middle. How most older film cameras measure light.
      Spot
      Measures a tiny area. Useful for a person on stage under a spotlight, or someone against a bright window.
      White balance (WB)

      Different light has different colours: indoor bulbs are orange, shade is blue. White balance corrects this so white things look white. Auto (AWB) works well most of the time. Try Daylight or Cloudy at sunset to keep warm, golden colours instead of having them "corrected" away.

      Film doesn't have white balance. Normal colour film is made for daylight, which is why old indoor photos look orange.

      Auto ISO

      Lets the camera raise ISO only when it needs to. Paired with A or S mode, it's a great beginner setup. In the menu, set a maximum ISO (3200 or 6400 is sensible) and a minimum shutter speed (1/125 for people, 1/500 for kids and pets).

      Drive mode: single, continuous, self-timer

      Single takes one photo per press. Continuous keeps shooting while you hold the button: great for action, then pick the best frame. Self-timer (2s or 10s) stops you shaking a camera on a tripod, or lets you get in the photo.

      RAW vs JPEG

      JPEG files are finished photos, small and ready to share. RAW files hold everything the sensor saw, so you can fix brightness and colour much more later, but they need editing software (Lightroom, Capture One, or the free Darktable or your camera maker's app).

      Not sure? Choose RAW + JPEG. You get a photo ready to share, plus a safety net for the special ones.

      Image stabilisation (IS, VR, OSS, IBIS)

      Counteracts your hands shaking so you can use slower shutter speeds by hand. Leave it on, except when the camera is on a tripod, where some older systems can actually add blur. Stabilisation doesn't freeze a moving subject; only a fast shutter does that.

      The histogram

      The little mountain graph on your screen shows how many parts of the photo are dark (left) through bright (right). If it's crammed against the right edge, highlights are lost to pure white. Crammed to the left, shadows are lost to black. It's more trustworthy than the screen, which can look brighter or darker depending on the room you're in.

      Flash

      The pop-up flash fires straight at people, which looks flat and harsh. Try raising ISO and opening the aperture first. If you do need flash, it's most useful outdoors in bright sun, to fill in dark shadows under hats and eyes. An add-on flash you can point at the ceiling ("bouncing") looks far more natural.

      Shooting film

      Film cameras work on exactly the same three controls. The big differences: ISO is fixed by the roll you load, you can't check your shots until they're developed, and many older cameras leave every decision to you.

      Choosing film

      ISO 100–200
      Bright sunny days. Fine grain, rich colour.
      ISO 400
      The all-rounder. Sun, cloud, shade and bright indoors. Best choice for a first roll.
      ISO 800–3200
      Dim light and night. More grain, which many people love.
      Colour negative
      The usual kind. Very forgiving: it handles overexposure well, so if unsure, give it more light rather than less.
      Black and white
      Forgiving too, and a good way to learn about light without colour distracting you.
      Slide film (E-6)
      Gorgeous colours but very strict about exposure. Save it for once you're confident.

      35mm is the common small roll (24 or 36 shots). 120 is wider "medium format" film used by bigger cameras, with 8–16 shots per roll.

      Loading a 35mm camera

      1. Open the back in shade, not direct sun. Pull up the rewind knob to open it on most cameras.
      2. Drop the canister in on the left, push the rewind knob back down.
      3. Pull the film leader across and tuck it into a slot on the take-up spool on the right. Make sure the sprocket holes sit on the teeth.
      4. Wind on and press the shutter once, then close the back.
      5. Wind and fire twice more until the counter reads 1.
      6. Check it's actually loaded: as you wind, the rewind knob should turn. If it doesn't, the film isn't moving.
      7. Set the film's ISO on the camera's ISO/ASA dial, if it has one, so the light meter is accurate. Modern cameras read it automatically from the canister.

      Finishing a roll

      When the lever won't wind any further, don't open the back. Press the small rewind release button (usually on the bottom), then turn the rewind crank until you feel it go slack. Now it's safe to open. Opening early will expose and ruin the film.

      The light meter

      Many film cameras have a built-in meter shown as a needle or small lights in the viewfinder. Change the aperture or shutter until it sits in the middle or the green light shows. Most need a small battery, so if nothing moves, check that first. Some very old cameras were designed for mercury batteries that aren't made any more; adapters and substitute cells exist.

      No meter, or not sure it works? Use a free light meter app on your phone, or the Sunny 16 rule below.

      On a sunny day, set the aperture to f/16 and the shutter to 1 over your film's ISO. Then adjust for the weather. Try it:

      Manual focus on film cameras

      Many film SLRs have a split-image circle in the middle of the viewfinder. Line it up on an edge, like a doorframe or someone's eyes, and turn the focus ring until the two halves join up. Rangefinder cameras do something similar with a ghost image you overlap.

      Getting it developed

      Any photo lab that does film can develop and scan it for you, usually within a week. Ask for scans to be emailed. Keep your negatives somewhere cool and dry; they're your originals.

      Checking a second-hand film camera: open the back, set the slowest shutter speed and fire it while looking through. It should open and close cleanly. Then try a fast speed. Check the lens for haze or fungus with a torch, and that the aperture blades are dry and snappy, not oily.

      Taking better photos

      Settings get a photo technically right. These habits make it worth looking at.

      Look at the light first

      The hour after sunrise and before sunset (golden hour) gives soft, warm light that flatters everything. Harsh midday sun makes squinting faces and hard shadows; move people into shade. Side light shows texture; light from behind makes glowing outlines.

      Get closer

      Most beginner photos have too much empty space. Fill the frame with what you care about. If it doesn't feel too close, take a step in.

      Use the rule of thirds

      Imagine the frame split into a 3×3 grid (turn on grid lines in your camera's menu). Put your subject or the horizon along those lines instead of dead centre. It's a guide, not a law: symmetry belongs in the centre.

      Check the edges and the background

      Before you press, glance around the frame. Poles growing out of heads, bins, half a stranger, a bright sign: move a step sideways and most of them disappear.

      Focus on the eyes

      For people and animals, the nearest eye should be sharpest. If the eyes are sharp, the photo feels sharp.

      Change your height

      Crouch to the eye level of kids and pets. Shoot from low to make things look grand, from above for food and patterns.

      Use lines to lead the eye

      Roads, fences, rivers and shorelines pull the viewer into the picture. Point them towards your subject.

      Hold the camera steady

      Elbows tucked in, left hand under the lens, look through the viewfinder rather than holding the camera at arm's length. Breathe out, then press gently. Keep the shutter at least as fast as 1 over your focal length: 1/50 at 50mm, 1/200 at 200mm.

      Keep the horizon straight

      A tilted sea or skyline is the easiest mistake to spot and the easiest to fix. The grid lines help here too.

      Take more, then choose

      Professionals keep a small fraction of what they shoot. Try a few angles, step closer, wait for the moment. Then be ruthless about which ones you share.

      A two-week practice plan

      One small exercise a day builds real skill faster than reading.

      DaysExercise
      1–2Shoot one object at f/1.8 (or your widest) and f/11. Compare the backgrounds.
      3–4Photograph moving water or traffic at 1/1000, 1/60 and 1/4.
      5–6Take the same indoor shot at ISO 200, 1600 and 6400. Zoom in on the noise.
      7–8Spend a day in A mode only. Note when the shutter speed gets too slow.
      9–10Use one focal length all day. Zoom with your feet.
      11–12Go out at golden hour and at midday. Compare the light.
      13–14Shoot a full scene in M mode, using the meter to guide you.

      When something goes wrong

      Everything is blurry
      Shutter too slow for your hands. Raise ISO or open the aperture so the shutter gets faster.
      The background is sharp but the person is soft
      The camera focused on the wrong thing. Use a single focus point on the eye.
      The moving subject is smeared
      Use S mode at 1/500 or faster, and AF-C focus.
      Photos are too dark or too bright
      Use exposure compensation, or check the meter in M mode.
      Grainy, speckled photos
      ISO is high. Find more light, open the aperture, or accept it; a grainy sharp photo beats a clean blurry one.
      The sky is pure white
      It's much brighter than the ground. Use −0.7 exposure comp, or put the sun behind you.
      Orange indoor photos
      Set white balance to Auto or Tungsten.
      Settings names differ slightly between brands: Canon uses Av and Tv where Nikon, Sony and Fujifilm use A and S. Your camera's manual has the exact button for each.
