Exposure basics

Your camera only does three things to light: lets in a certain amount, for a certain time, and records it with a certain sensitivity. Exposure is how bright the result comes out. Each setting also changes how the photo looks, and that's the real reason to learn them.

Aperture: how wide the hole is

Inside the lens is an opening that grows and shrinks, like the pupil of your eye. It's written as an f-number: f/1.8, f/4, f/8, f/16. The numbers work backwards: small number = big opening = more light.

What it changes in the picture: how much is in focus. Wide apertures (f/1.4–f/2.8) blur the background. Narrow ones (f/8–f/16) keep everything sharp.

f/2.8

Shutter speed: how long the light gets in

The shutter opens and closes. The time is written as a fraction of a second (1/1000 is quick, 1/30 is slow) or in whole seconds with a quote mark (2" is two seconds).

What it changes in the picture: movement. Fast shutters freeze action. Slow shutters blur it, which can look broken (shaky hands) or beautiful (silky water, light trails).

Shutter speedWhat it does well
1/4000–1/2000Birds in flight, splashing water, fast sport in bright sun
1/1000–1/500Running kids and pets, cars, most sport
1/250–1/125People walking, everyday photos, general use
1/60–1/30Still subjects by hand indoors; the slowest most people can hold steady
1/15–1/2Panning with moving subjects, slightly blurred water, needs a steady rest
1"–30"Silky waterfalls, light trails, night cities, stars: tripod only

ISO: how sensitive the camera is

ISO is how strongly the camera amplifies the light it receives. Low ISO (100–200) is clean; high ISO (3200+) is brighter but grainier.

What it changes in the picture: noise, or grain. Raise it only when you can't open the aperture wider or slow the shutter further. A sharp grainy photo beats a clean blurry one.

On film, ISO is set by the roll you load. ISO 400 film is ISO 400 for the whole roll.

Stops: the one idea that ties it together

A stop means doubling or halving the light. 1/250 → 1/125 is one stop more. So is f/5.6 → f/4, or ISO 200 → ISO 400. Because they share the same unit, you can trade between them: shutter one stop faster, aperture one stop wider, and the brightness stays the same.

Full aperture stops: f/1.4, 2, 2.8, 4, 5.6, 8, 11, 16, 22. Each lets in half the light of the one before. Cameras also show the thirds in between, like f/3.2 and f/3.5.

Why the camera gets it wrong sometimes

The light meter assumes the world averages out to a middle grey. That works most of the time, but snow and white walls get made too dark (grey snow), and black cats or night scenes get made too bright. When that happens, use exposure compensation to tell the camera "brighter" or "darker".

The mode dial

The mode dial decides who makes which decision. Each step from Auto towards M hands one more choice from the camera to you. Tap a mode to turn the dial.

Which mode should a beginner use?

Start in A (Av on Canon). You pick the aperture, which decides the look of the photo, and the camera handles the shutter speed. Glance at the shutter number: if it drops below 1/60, raise the ISO.

Once that feels natural, use S for anything that moves, and M when the light isn't changing, like a sunset or a night scene on a tripod.

Custom modes (C1, C2, U1…)

Some cameras have custom positions that save a whole set of settings. Set up your favourite sport settings once, save them to C1, and switch to them in one turn.

Adapting lenses

Mirrorless cameras can usually take older lenses with an adapter. Canon's EF-to-RF and Nikon's F-to-Z adapters keep autofocus with most lenses. Cheap adapters for old manual film lenses work too, as long as you focus by hand and set the aperture on the lens. It's a fun, cheap way to use vintage glass.

Learn your own camera faster

  • Download the manual as a PDF to your phone and search it for the word you need.
  • Set up the quick menu (Q, i or Fn) with the six settings you change most.
  • Assign the custom buttons to things you use often, like ISO or the focus mode.
  • Most cameras have a "My Menu" page: put your favourite settings there.

Lenses and focal length

Focal length is the number in millimetres on your lens, like 18–55mm or 50mm. Smaller number = wider view. Bigger number = closer, narrower view. Drag the slider.

50mm

Sensor size and "crop factor"

Full-frame sensors are the same size as a frame of 35mm film. Smaller sensors see only the middle of what the lens projects, so every lens looks more zoomed in.

SensorCrop factorA 50mm lens looks likeFound in

APS-C1.5× (Canon 1.6×)75–80mmMost entry and mid-level cameras
Micro Four Thirds2×100mmOM System/Olympus, Panasonic
1-inchabout 2.7×about 135mmPremium compacts

Bigger sensors generally mean cleaner high-ISO photos and blurrier backgrounds at the same framing. Smaller sensors mean smaller, lighter gear and extra "reach" for wildlife.

Zoom lenses and prime lenses

Zoom (18–55mm, 70–300mm)

Covers many focal lengths. Flexible, but usually doesn't open as wide, so it needs more light.

Prime (35mm, 50mm f/1.8)

One fixed focal length; zoom with your feet. Usually sharper and much brighter. A 50mm f/1.8 is the best-value upgrade for most beginners.

Reading a lens

18–55mm 1:3.5–5.6 zooms from 18 to 55mm; widest aperture is f/3.5 at 18mm, shrinking to f/5.6 zoomed in. 50mm 1:1.8 is a prime that opens to f/1.8. IS, VR, OSS, OIS mean built-in stabilisation. Macro means it focuses very close. Ø58 is the filter thread size in millimetres.

Special lens types

Macro
Focuses close enough to fill the frame with a coin or an insect.
Fisheye
Extremely wide with curved, bubble-like distortion.
Superzoom (e.g. 18–200mm)
One lens for everything; great for travel, softer and darker than specialised lenses.
Teleconverter
Fits between camera and telephoto lens to multiply its reach (1.4× or 2×), costing one or two stops of light.
Tilt-shift
Keeps building lines straight, or creates the "miniature" effect.

Focal length and faces

Wide lenses up close stretch features: noses look bigger, foreheads bulge. For flattering portraits, step back and use 50–135mm. It isn't the lens causing it; it's how close you're standing.

Focus and sharpness

Blurry photos have one of three causes: missed focus, camera shake or subject movement. Learn to tell them apart and you can fix every one.

What you seeCauseFix
Something else is sharp, just not your subjectMissed focusSingle focus point on the subject, or eye detection
Everything is smeared in the same directionCamera shakeFaster shutter, stabilisation, tripod, better grip
Background is sharp, moving subject is smearedSubject movementFaster shutter (1/500+)
Nothing is quite sharp anywhereDirty lens, cheap filter, or extreme apertureClean the lens, remove the filter, use f/5.6–f/8

The half-press

Press the shutter button halfway: the camera focuses and measures light. Press fully to take the photo. Jabbing it straight down is the most common cause of blur.

Focus modes

AF-S / One Shot
Focuses once and locks. For still subjects.
AF-C / AI Servo
Keeps tracking while you hold the half-press. For anything that moves.
AF-A / AI Focus
Camera guesses which you need. Fine, but less predictable.
MF
You turn the focus ring. For low light, through glass, or close-ups. Use magnification or focus peaking on screen to help.

Focus areas

Single point
You choose exactly where. Most reliable for still subjects.
Zone / group
A cluster of points. Good for birds and sport where the subject moves around a bit.
Wide / auto area
Camera chooses, usually picking the closest thing. Fine with face or eye detection on.
Tracking
You tell it what to follow, and it follows that subject around the frame.

Focus and recompose, or back-button focus

To focus on something off-centre with a centre point: half-press on the subject, keep holding, move the camera to frame, then press fully. Many enthusiasts move focus to the AF-ON button on the back instead, so the shutter button only takes photos. Hold AF-ON to track, release it to lock.

What decides depth of field

Depth of field is how much of the scene from front to back looks sharp. Four things change it:

  • Aperture: wider (f/1.8) = shallower.
  • Distance: closer to the subject = shallower.
  • Focal length: longer = looks shallower at the same framing, with background more blurred.
  • Background distance: move the subject away from the background for more blur.

Holding steady

  • Shutter at least 1 over your focal length: 1/50 at 50mm, 1/200 at 200mm (multiply by the crop factor on APS-C).
  • Stabilisation can give you 2–5 extra stops. It fixes shake, never subject movement.
  • Elbows tucked in, left hand cradling the lens, camera against your face.
  • Breathe out, then squeeze the button. Shoot a short burst; one frame is usually sharpest.
  • Lean on walls, rest on railings, use a beanbag, or a 2-second timer on a tripod.

Menu settings explained

Menus are full of abbreviations. These are the ones that matter. Tap any to open it.

Exposure compensation (+/−)

Tells the camera "brighter than you think" or "darker". Works in P, A, S, Auto ISO and many Auto modes. +1 is twice as bright.

Snow and white sand: +1. Sunsets and dark scenes: −0.7 to −1. Set it back to 0 afterwards.

Metering: matrix, centre-weighted, spot
Matrix / Evaluative / Multi
Measures the whole frame and makes a smart guess. Leave it here most of the time.
Centre-weighted
Mostly the middle. How most older film cameras meter.
Spot
A tiny area only. For a person on stage under a spotlight or against a bright window.
Highlight-weighted
Protects the brightest parts. Great for concerts and stage lighting.
White balance (WB)

Light comes in different colours. White balance corrects it so white looks white. Auto works well; try Daylight at sunset to keep warm colours. The Light lesson has a live demo.

Auto ISO

Raises ISO only when needed. With A or S mode it's the ideal beginner setup. In the menu, set a maximum ISO (3200–6400) and a minimum shutter speed (1/125 for people, 1/500 for kids and pets).

Drive mode

Single: one photo per press. Continuous (L/H): keeps shooting while held; pick the best later. Self-timer 2s/10s: no shake on a tripod, or get in the photo. Interval timer: for time-lapses.

Image quality: RAW vs JPEG

JPEG: finished, small, ready to share. RAW: everything the sensor saw, so you can fix brightness and colour far more later, but needs editing software. Not sure? Choose RAW + JPEG.

Set JPEG to Large / Fine. There's no reason to save smaller files on today's cards.

Picture style / Picture control / Film simulation

Changes how JPEGs look: Standard, Vivid, Portrait, Monochrome, and on Fujifilm, film simulations. They don't affect RAW files, which you can restyle later.

Image stabilisation (IS, VR, OSS, IBIS)

Counteracts hand shake so you can use slower shutters. IBIS is in the body, the others in the lens. Leave it on handheld; some systems should be switched off on a tripod (check your manual). It doesn't freeze moving subjects.

The histogram

The mountain graph shows dark tones on the left and bright on the right. Crammed against the right edge = highlights lost to white. Crammed left = shadows lost to black. Trust it over the screen, which looks different in sun and in a dark room. The Playground has a live one.

Highlight alert ("blinkies")

Turn this on in playback settings. Areas that are pure white will flash. If something important is blinking, use exposure compensation to darken.

Silent / electronic shutter

Mirrorless cameras can take photos with no moving shutter: silent, and often very fast. Downsides: fast-moving things can look bent ("rolling shutter"), and flickering lights can cause banding. Use the mechanical shutter for sport and under artificial lights.

Long exposure noise reduction

After a long exposure, the camera takes a second dark frame of equal length to subtract noise. Useful, but it doubles your wait. Turn it off for star photos and time-lapses.

Memory cards and formatting

Format new cards in the camera, not on a computer. Don't remove the card while the light is blinking. For video, look for a V30 or U3 rating. A few smaller cards are safer than one huge one: a lost card isn't a lost trip.

Date, time and copyright

Set the clock correctly (and update it when travelling) so photos sort properly. Many cameras let you enter your name in a copyright field that's saved in every file.

Understanding light

The best photographers aren't looking at subjects, they're looking at light. Four things describe any light: its direction, quality (hard or soft), colour and strength.

Direction

Move the light around the subject. The small diagram shows where the light is compared with the camera.

Hard and soft

Hard light comes from a small, bright source like the midday sun or a bare flash. It makes crisp, dark shadows: dramatic, but unflattering on faces. Soft light comes from a big source: a cloudy sky, a large window, a flash bounced off the ceiling. Shadows fade gently, which flatters almost everything. The bigger and closer the light, the softer it gets.

Time of day

Golden hour
Roughly the hour after sunrise and before sunset. Warm, low, soft light with long shadows. The favourite time for portraits and landscapes.
Blue hour
The 20–40 minutes after sunset (or before sunrise) when the sky turns deep blue. Perfect for cities with lights on.
Midday
Harsh, overhead light. Move people into open shade, or shoot architecture and strong graphic shapes.
Overcast
A giant softbox in the sky. Great for portraits, waterfalls, forests and flowers; boring skies, so leave them out of frame.

Colour of light and white balance

Light colour is measured in kelvin (K): low numbers are orange, high numbers are blue. Your camera's white balance setting tries to cancel it out. Try mismatching them:

Flash

  • Pop-up flash straight on looks flat with harsh shadows behind people. Try raising ISO first.
  • Bounce flash: an add-on flash pointed at a white ceiling or wall turns a small hard light into a big soft one.
  • Fill flash: outdoors in sun, a little flash lifts dark shadows under hats and eyes. Use flash compensation around −1.
  • Sync speed: most cameras can't use flash faster than about 1/200 or 1/250 unless you turn on high-speed sync (HSS).
  • Slow sync / night portrait: flash freezes the person while a slow shutter lets the city lights in behind.
  • Red-eye comes from flash close to the lens. Bounce it, or ask people not to look straight into it.

Free light shapers

A white sheet of card bounces light into shadows. Kitchen foil works as a hard reflector. A sheer curtain softens a sunny window. Someone in a white shirt standing just out of frame is a reflector too.

Composition

Composition is where you put things in the frame. Settings make a photo correct; composition makes it interesting.

Try it: drag the horizon and the boat

Drag the boat anywhere. Drag the sea up or down to move the horizon.

    Twelve ideas to try

    Rule of thirds
    Put the subject or horizon on the lines a third of the way in. Turn on the grid in your camera's display settings.
    Fill the frame
    Get closer than feels natural. Most beginner photos have too much empty space.
    Simplify
    Decide what the photo is about and remove everything that isn't helping.
    Check the edges
    Poles out of heads, bins, half a stranger, bright signs. One step sideways fixes most of them.
    Leading lines
    Roads, fences, rivers and shorelines pull the eye towards your subject.
    Give movement room
    Leave space in front of a moving subject or someone's gaze, not behind.
    Frame within a frame
    Shoot through doorways, windows, arches or branches.
    Foreground interest
    In landscapes, put a rock or flowers near the bottom to add depth.
    Symmetry and patterns
    Break the thirds rule: symmetry belongs in the centre. A pattern with one thing breaking it is even better.
    Change your height
    Crouch to kids' and pets' eye level. Shoot from low to make things grand, from above for food and flat lays.
    Negative space
    A small subject in a big empty sky or wall can feel calm and powerful.
    Straight horizons
    A tilted sea is the easiest mistake to spot. Use the grid or the camera's level.

    Vertical or horizontal?

    Turn the camera to match the subject: tall things (people, trees, buildings) suit vertical; wide things (beaches, groups, skylines) suit horizontal. When in doubt, take both.

    Shooting film

    Film cameras use exactly the same three controls. The differences: ISO is fixed by the roll, you can't check shots until they're developed, and many older cameras leave every decision to you.

    Choosing film

    ISO 100–200
    Bright sunny days. Fine grain, rich colour.
    ISO 400
    The all-rounder for sun, cloud, shade and bright indoors. Best first roll.
    ISO 800–3200
    Dim light and night. More grain, which many people love.
    Colour negative (C-41)
    The usual kind. Very forgiving of overexposure: if unsure, give it more light.
    Black and white
    Forgiving, cheap to develop at home, great for learning light.
    Slide film (E-6)
    Stunning colour, but strict about exposure. For once you're confident.

    Formats

    35mm
    The common small roll: 24 or 36 shots. Fits most film SLRs and point-and-shoots.
    Half-frame
    35mm film, but each shot uses half a frame: 72 shots per 36-exposure roll.
    120 (medium format)
    Wider paper-backed film: 8 to 16 shots depending on the camera (6×9, 6×7, 6×6, 6×4.5). Much more detail.
    Instant
    Polaroid and Instax print straight out of the camera.

    Loading 35mm

    1. Open the back in shade. On most cameras, pull up the rewind knob.
    2. Drop the canister in on the left, push the knob back down.
    3. Tuck the leader into the take-up spool, with the sprocket holes on the teeth.
    4. Wind and fire once, close the back, then wind and fire until the counter shows 1.
    5. Check the rewind knob turns as you wind. If it doesn't, the film isn't moving.
    6. Set the film's ISO on the camera's ISO/ASA dial if it has one.

    Finishing a roll

    When the lever won't wind any further, don't open the back. Press the rewind release button underneath, turn the crank until it goes slack, then open. Opening early ruins the film.

    Metering on film cameras

    Built-in meters show a needle or lights in the viewfinder; balance them in the middle. Most need a small battery, so check that first if nothing moves. Some old cameras were designed for mercury batteries that aren't made any more; adapters and substitute cells exist. No meter? Use a phone light meter app or the Sunny 16 helper.

    Manual focus aids

    Film SLRs often have a split-image circle: line it up on an edge and turn the ring until the halves join. Rangefinders show a ghost image to overlap. Many lenses have a depth of field scale: for street photography, set f/8 and focus at about 3m, and roughly 1.5m to 10m will be acceptably sharp without refocusing ("zone focusing").

    Push and pull

    You can rate film at a different ISO, then tell the lab. Shooting ISO 400 film at 1600 is a "two-stop push": the lab develops longer to compensate. More contrast and grain, but usable in the dark. Pulling is the reverse. Always rate the whole roll at the same speed.

    Developing

    Any photo lab that handles film can develop and scan it, usually within a week. Ask for scans emailed and keep your negatives somewhere cool and dry. Black and white is easy to develop at home with a developing tank, a changing bag and three chemicals: developer, stop and fixer.

    Long exposures on film need extra time beyond what the meter says (reciprocity failure). The reciprocity calculator works it out.

    Checking a second-hand film camera: open the back and fire at the slowest speed while looking through. It should open and close cleanly. Try a fast speed too. Shine a torch through the lens for haze or fungus, and check the aperture blades are dry and snappy, not oily. Run a cheap roll through before trusting it with something important.

    Editing basics

    Almost every great photo is edited a little. Every editing app, from your phone to Lightroom, uses the same core sliders. Try them here.

    Tone curve: how dark tones (left) through bright tones (right) are being changed

    A simple editing order

    1. Crop and straighten. Fix the horizon, tighten the framing.
    2. Exposure. Get the overall brightness right.
    3. Highlights and shadows. Pull back bright skies, lift dark faces.
    4. Contrast. A little adds punch.
    5. White balance. Warmer or cooler to taste.
    6. Colour. Vibrance is gentler than saturation, especially on skin.
    7. Final touches. Spot-remove dust, light sharpening, noise reduction for high-ISO shots.

    The best editing advice: when you think it looks right, pull every slider back a little. Over-editing is the most common beginner mistake, and it dates a photo fast.

    Apps to start with

    Free: your phone's built-in editor, Snapseed, Darktable, RawTherapee, and your camera maker's own software. Paid: Adobe Lightroom, Capture One, DxO PhotoLab, Affinity Photo. Lightroom-style apps also organise your library, which matters more as it grows.

    Backing up

    Follow the 3-2-1 rule: three copies of your photos, on two different kinds of storage, with one copy somewhere else (cloud, or a drive at a relative's house). Memory cards and single hard drives do fail.

    Video basics

    Video uses the same aperture and ISO you already know. The big difference is that shutter speed is mostly decided for you by the frame rate.

    Frame rate

    24 or 25 fps
    The classic film look. Use 25 in Australia and other countries with 50Hz power so indoor lights don't flicker.
    30 fps
    Common in the US, Canada and Japan (60Hz power).
    50 or 60 fps
    Smoother motion for sport and action, and can be slowed to half speed.
    100 or 120 fps
    For slow motion. Slowed down to 25 fps, it plays four or five times slower.

    The 180° shutter rule

    Set the shutter to about double the frame rate: 1/50 for 25 fps, 1/100 for 50 fps. Motion then has a natural, gentle blur. Much faster shutters look jittery, like a stuttery action scene.

    That's a problem in bright sun: with the shutter fixed at 1/50, the only way to avoid overexposure is a narrow aperture, which loses background blur. The answer is an ND filter. A variable ND, which you twist to adjust, is the favourite for video.

    shutter ND needed at ISO 100

    Resolution and settings

    • 4K is sharper and lets you crop later. 1080p files are smaller and easier to edit on older computers.
    • Use AF-C with face or eye tracking, or manual focus for steady scenes.
    • Turn on stabilisation (or "Active/Enhanced" digital stabilisation) for handheld shots.
    • Log profiles (C-Log, N-Log, S-Log, F-Log) keep more detail for colour grading, but look flat and grey until edited. Skip them until you're comfortable editing.
    • Use a fast card: V30 for most 4K, V60 or V90 for high-bitrate modes.
    • Long 4K clips can heat cameras up. Check for recording limits and overheating settings.

    Sound matters more than picture

    People forgive soft footage, but not bad audio. A small on-camera microphone is the biggest upgrade, and a clip-on lavalier mic or wireless kit is best for talking to camera.

    • Use a fluffy "dead cat" wind cover outdoors.
    • Get the mic close to the speaker's mouth.
    • Set audio levels so speech peaks around −12dB, never touching 0.
    • Listen with headphones if your camera has a headphone socket.

    Shooting tips

    • Hold each shot for at least 10 seconds; you'll need the extra when editing.
    • Move slowly: smooth pans and walking shots, or use a tripod or gimbal.
    • Shoot "B-roll": hands, details, wide shots and reactions to cut away to.
    • Wide, medium and close-up of the same moment makes editing far easier.
    • Face your speaker towards a window for soft, flattering light.

    Getting more from a phone camera

    Everything in this course applies to phones too. The differences: phones have a fixed aperture, so they control light with only shutter speed and ISO, and they lean heavily on processing to make up for their small sensors.

    Know your lenses

    The zoom buttons (0.5×, 1×, 3× and so on) switch between separate cameras.

    ButtonWhat it isUse it for
    0.5× (ultra-wide)Roughly a 13–16mm equivalentBig landscapes, small rooms, dramatic close angles. Avoid for faces near the edges.
    1× (main)Roughly 24–26mm; the biggest, best sensorAlmost everything, and especially low light.
    2×Usually a crop from the main cameraPortraits and everyday shots with a more natural look.
    3×–5× (telephoto)A separate telephoto lens, roughly 70–120mmPortraits, details, distant subjects in good light.
    Beyond thatDigital zoom: cropping and enlargingQuality drops fast. Walk closer instead.

    Which lenses and numbers you have depends on your phone. In dim light, many phones quietly switch from telephoto to a crop of the main camera.

    Ten habits that make a big difference

    1. Wipe the lens. Pocket grease is the number one cause of hazy, glowing phone photos.
    2. Tap to focus on your subject, then slide the brightness up or down. That's exposure compensation.
    3. Long-press to lock focus and exposure (AE/AF lock) so they don't change as you recompose.
    4. Turn on the grid and level in camera settings for straight horizons and thirds.
    5. Hold still for night mode. It takes several seconds of frames; brace on something.
    6. Use burst or Live Photos for moving kids and pets, then pick the best frame.
    7. Use the volume button as a shutter: it's steadier than tapping the screen.
    8. Don't over-zoom. Stick to the real lens buttons.
    9. Turn off the flash unless you really need it; night mode almost always looks better.
    10. Find good light. A phone in great light beats an expensive camera in bad light.

    Portrait mode

    Portrait mode fakes background blur with software. It's great for people, but watch for mistakes around hair, glasses, ears and fingers. Most phones let you adjust or remove the blur afterwards. Stand at the distance the camera asks for, and keep plenty of space behind your subject.

    Pro mode and RAW

    Many phones offer a Pro or Manual mode (or apps that add one) where you set ISO, shutter speed, white balance and focus yourself, plus RAW capture for editing. It's a great way to practise this course with the camera you always carry. Remember the aperture usually can't change, so ISO and shutter do all the work.

    Where a dedicated camera still wins

    • Fast action: kids' sport, pets running, birds in flight.
    • Real optical zoom past about 5×.
    • Natural background blur without software errors.
    • Low light without heavy processing, and large prints.
    • Swappable lenses, proper grip and controls.

    Handy phone accessories

    A small tripod with a phone clamp, a Bluetooth remote, a gimbal for smooth video, a clip-on mic, and a power bank. Clip-on lenses are hit and miss: try before you buy.

    Shooting guides

    Settings and habits for specific subjects. Tap one to open it.

    Portraits

    Mode: A/Av. Aperture: f/1.8–f/2.8 for one person, f/5.6–f/8 for groups. Lens: 50–135mm (full-frame equivalent). Focus: eye detection or a single point on the nearest eye.

    • Put people in open shade or with the sun behind them, and bounce light back onto their face.
    • Keep distance between them and the background for blur.
    • Talk to them. Real expressions come between the posed shots.
    • Avoid cropping at joints (wrists, knees, ankles).
    Landscapes

    Mode: A/Av. Aperture: f/8–f/11. ISO: 100. Focus: about a third into the scene, or the hyperfocal distance from the DoF calculator.

    • Shoot at golden hour; midday is flat.
    • Add foreground interest.
    • A tripod lets you keep low ISO and narrow apertures as the light fades.
    • A polarising filter deepens blue skies and cuts reflections on water.
    Kids and pets

    Mode: S/Tv at 1/500, or A/Av with Auto ISO and a 1/500 minimum. Focus: AF-C with eye/animal detection. Drive: continuous.

    • Get down to their eye level.
    • Shoot during play, not posed. Hold a treat or toy right next to the lens.
    • A big window indoors beats flash every time.
    Sport and action

    Mode: S/Tv, or M with Auto ISO. Shutter: 1/1000 or faster for fast sport; 1/500 for running. Focus: AF-C, zone or tracking. Drive: high-speed continuous.

    • Know the game so you can anticipate where the action goes.
    • Pre-focus on a spot where it will happen (the goal, the finish line).
    • Panning: follow a moving subject at 1/30–1/60 for a sharp subject on a streaky background.
    Wildlife and birds

    Lens: 300mm or more. Shutter: 1/1000+ for small birds, 1/2000+ in flight. Mode: M with Auto ISO, or S. Focus: AF-C with animal detection.

    • Early morning is best for activity and light.
    • Focus on the eye; a sharp eye makes the shot.
    • Be patient and quiet; let animals come to you, and never disturb nests.
    Cities at night

    Mode: M on a tripod. Settings: f/8, ISO 100, 2–15 seconds. Timer: 2s.

    • Blue hour beats full darkness: the sky still has colour.
    • Long shutter speeds turn traffic into light trails.
    • No tripod? Brace on something solid, f/1.8, ISO 3200, 1/30.
    Stars and the Milky Way

    Settings: widest aperture (f/2.8 or wider is ideal), ISO 3200–6400, shutter from the night sky calculator (usually 10–25 seconds). Widest lens you have. Tripod essential.

    • Go somewhere dark, away from city lights, with no moon.
    • Manual focus: use live view, magnify a bright star, turn until it's smallest.
    • Turn off long exposure noise reduction and stabilisation.
    Fireworks

    Settings: M, f/8–f/11, ISO 100, Bulb or 2–6 seconds. Manual focus on infinity. Tripod and remote release.

    Open the shutter on the launch thump, close it after the burst. Stand upwind so the smoke blows away from you.

    Travel and street

    Lens: 24–35mm (equivalent), or one small zoom. Mode: A/Av with Auto ISO, f/5.6–f/8.

    • Mix wide scenes, medium shots of people, and close details (food, textures, signs) to tell the full story.
    • Zone focus for quick candid shots.
    • Be respectful: ask before close portraits, and follow local customs about photography.
    Food

    Light: a window to the side or behind the plate. Angle: straight above for flat dishes, about 45° for burgers and drinks. Aperture: f/2.8–f/5.6.

    Turn off overhead lights to avoid mixed colours. A white card on the opposite side fills shadows.

    Close-ups and macro

    Aperture: f/8–f/16, because depth of field is razor-thin up close. Focus: manual, then rock gently forward and back. Shoot several and keep the sharpest.

    Wind ruins macro: shoot early in the morning, or block the breeze. No macro lens? Try extension tubes or a close-up filter.

    Indoor events and concerts

    Lens: the brightest you own (an f/1.8 prime is ideal). Mode: A/Av or M with Auto ISO, shutter 1/125+, ISO up to 6400. Metering: spot or highlight-weighted for stage lights.

    Use the silent shutter where allowed. Check whether cameras are permitted before you go.

    Products and things to sell online

    Soft window light, a plain white or neutral background (a sheet of card curved up behind the object), f/8, tripod, ISO 100, 2s timer. Show the item from several angles plus close-ups of any details or flaws.

        Checklist for a night out

        • Tripod, remote or 2s timer, spare batteries (cold drains them fast).
        • Head torch with a red-light mode to keep your night vision.
        • Check the moon phase and cloud forecast; new moon is darkest.
        • Turn screen brightness down, and turn off stabilisation and long exposure noise reduction.
        • Focus manually on a bright star using magnified live view.

        Troubleshooting

        Everything is blurry
        Shutter too slow for your hands. Raise ISO or open the aperture so the shutter gets faster.
        The background is sharp but the person isn't
        Wrong focus point. Use a single point on the eye, or turn on eye detection.
        Moving subjects are smeared
        S mode at 1/500 or faster, with AF-C.
        Too dark or too bright
        Exposure compensation, or check the meter in M mode.
        Grainy, speckled photos
        High ISO. Find more light, open the aperture, or accept it.
        Sky is pure white
        Use −0.7 exposure compensation, put the sun behind you, or use a graduated filter.
        Orange indoor photos
        White balance to Auto or Tungsten.
        Dark spots in the same place on every photo
        Dust on the sensor, most visible at f/11 and above. See Care and cleaning.
        Camera won't take a photo
        It can't focus (too close, too dark, plain surface), the card is full or locked, or the flash is charging. Switch to MF to test.
        Sharp on the camera screen, soft on the computer
        The screen is small and forgiving. Zoom in on playback to check focus before you leave.
        Lines or bands across photos indoors
        Flickering lights with the electronic shutter. Use the mechanical shutter, or a shutter speed like 1/50 or 1/60.
        Viewfinder is blurry but photos are sharp
        Adjust the dioptre wheel beside the viewfinder to suit your eyesight.
        Film: blank roll
        The film didn't catch the take-up spool (check the rewind knob turns), or the lens cap was left on a rangefinder.
        Film: part of the frame is black
        Shutter faster than the flash sync speed, or a shutter fault that needs a repair shop.
        Film: orange or red streaks
        A light leak, often worn foam seals around the back door. Cheap to replace.
        Film: thin, dark scans
        Underexposed. Check the meter battery and ISO setting. Colour negative prefers too much light over too little.

        Reference

        Buying gear

        What kind of camera?

        Mirrorless
        Where nearly all new models are now. The electronic viewfinder shows the actual exposure, which makes learning easier. Smaller bodies and excellent subject-detection autofocus.
        DSLR
        Uses a mirror and optical viewfinder. Great value second-hand, with long battery life and huge lens choice, though few new models are being made.
        Premium compact
        Pocketable with a bigger sensor than a phone. Ideal for travel.
        Bridge / superzoom
        One body with a huge built-in zoom. Good for wildlife on a budget.
        Phone
        Brilliant for everyday photos. A dedicated camera wins at fast action, natural background blur, long zoom and low light without heavy processing.

        What actually matters

        • How it feels in your hand. The camera you enjoy carrying gets used.
        • The lens system. You'll buy lenses for years; choose a mount with lenses you want at prices you can afford.
        • Autofocus if you shoot kids, pets, sport or wildlife.
        • Megapixels matter far less than you'd think. 20–26MP is plenty for almost everyone.
        • A flip or tilting screen helps with low and high angles, vlogging and selfies.
        • A viewfinder is much easier in bright sun than the rear screen.

        Lenses, in the order most people need them

        1. The kit zoom (around 18–55mm or 16–50mm). Keep it; learn with it.
        2. A fast 50mm or 35mm prime (f/1.8). Cheap, sharp, bright, background blur.
        3. A telephoto zoom (around 55–250mm or 70–300mm) for sport, wildlife and kids at the park.
        4. Then specialise: ultra-wide for landscapes and interiors, macro for close-ups, or an f/2.8 zoom for events.

        Third-party makers such as Sigma, Tamron and Viltrox make good-value lenses for most mounts. Double-check the mount before buying.

        Accessories worth having

        Spare battery
        The single most useful extra. Cold weather and video drain them fast.
        Good memory cards
        From a reputable brand. UHS-I U3/V30 is enough for most photo and 4K video; faster cards help with long bursts.
        Tripod
        For night, landscapes, long exposures and self-portraits. Check the weight rating covers your camera and biggest lens.
        Lens hood
        Reduces flare and protects the front of the lens. Often included; use it.
        Cleaning kit
        Air blower, soft brush, microfibre cloth.
        Comfortable strap and bag
        So the camera comes with you instead of sitting at home.
        Filters
        A circular polariser (deeper skies, fewer reflections) and an ND filter for long exposures. Clear "UV" filters are optional protection; cheap ones can soften photos.
        External flash
        One you can tilt and bounce off the ceiling transforms indoor photos.
        Remote release
        Or use the camera maker's phone app as a remote.

        Buying second-hand

        • Ask for the shutter count on digital bodies and compare it to the rated life.
        • Check the sensor for scratches, and the lens for haze, fungus and scratches. Test autofocus at both ends of a zoom.
        • Try every button, the card door and the screen, and check the battery holds charge.
        • Buy from a seller with returns or a warranty where possible.

        Starting with film

        An automatic 35mm point-and-shoot is the easiest start. A manual 35mm SLR with a working light meter teaches you the most. Budget for film and developing too: over time they cost more than the camera.

        Care and cleaning

        Cleaning a lens

        1. Blow off loose dust with an air blower first, so you don't grind grit into the glass.
        2. Brush away what's left with a soft lens brush.
        3. Wipe in gentle circles from the centre with a clean microfibre cloth, using a drop of lens cleaning fluid on the cloth (never directly on the lens).

        Don't use your shirt, tissues or household glass cleaner. A little dust on the front glass rarely shows in photos.

        Sensor dust

        • Change lenses quickly, with the camera facing down, out of the wind.
        • Use the camera's built-in sensor cleaning function.
        • Use a hand blower (never canned compressed air) with the camera facing down.
        • Don't touch the sensor. Wet cleaning swabs exist, but if you're unsure, a camera shop can do it.

        Weather and temperature

        • Going from cold air into a warm room, seal the camera in a bag first and let it warm up slowly to avoid condensation inside.
        • Cold drains batteries: keep spares in an inside pocket.
        • "Weather sealed" means resistant, not waterproof. A rain cover or even a shower cap is cheap insurance.
        • At the beach, keep sand and salt spray away, and wipe the camera down afterwards.
        • Don't leave cameras or film in a hot car.

        Storage

        • Store cameras and lenses somewhere dry with airflow. In humid weather, use silica gel or a dry cabinet to prevent lens fungus.
        • Take the battery out if storing for months.
        • Unused film keeps longest in the fridge. Let it warm up for an hour before opening the packet.
        • Get exposed film developed promptly.

        Batteries and cards

        • Use genuine or well-reviewed third-party batteries; poor ones can swell or fail.
        • Format cards in the camera after backing up, rather than deleting photos one by one.
        • Never pull a card or battery while the camera is writing.
